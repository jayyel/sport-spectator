The Dolphins didn't just lose this week. They lost the two players who were supposed to anchor the next two years of the rebuild — on the same day, for different reasons, with neither one coming back anytime soon.

On Tuesday, Miami placed quarterback Malik Willis on injured reserve and defensive tackle Zach Sieler on injured reserve. Sieler's injury is season-ending. Willis is out a minimum of four games. Kyle McCord — a second-year quarterback who has never thrown a regular-season pass in the NFL — gets the ball Sunday in Indianapolis.

## The Willis Bet Didn't Even Last a Season

Miami <cite index="24-6">signed Willis to a three-year, $67.5 million contract this offseason, with a plan to build around him over the next two seasons.</cite> He reunited with Hafley, who had coached him the year before in Green Bay. Through eight starts, the results were mixed at best: <cite index="22-12">six touchdowns and six interceptions while completing 58.2% of passes for 1,396 yards.</cite>

The injury had been building for weeks. <cite index="21-6,21-7">Willis has had a lingering groin injury that has been impacting his mobility, and an MRI showed it is now a core muscle injury.</cite> Hafley said the tape from the loss to Detroit made the decision obvious. <cite index="22-9">"You look back at Malik and you watch him in that last game and you could see it," Hafley said Wednesday.</cite> The injury isn't considered career- or season-threatening — <cite index="21-1">Willis' injury is not considered season-ending</cite> — but the Dolphins are now without the quarterback they handed a franchise-altering contract to just months ago, for at least a month and possibly longer.

## Losing Sieler Changes the Defense's Math

If Willis was the offseason's most expensive bet, Sieler was the defense's most reliable piece. He got hurt on the first snap of the season and played through it anyway. <cite index="61-1">Sieler tore his biceps muscle on the first play of the team's Week 9 game against the Detroit Lions, but played through the injury for the remainder of the game.</cite> The damage was worse than anyone realized in real time — <cite index="61-3">he will need surgery and around six months of recovery time before he is cleared to return to football activities.</cite>

Hafley didn't hide his respect for how Sieler handled it. <cite index="62-5">"I give him a ton of credit not only for pushing through that game and playing at a high level but just for everything he's done," Hafley said.</cite> Miami has already moved to patch the interior line, signing veteran Keith Cooper Jr. off Carolina's practice squad to fill the void left by Sieler's absence.

## McCord's Shot, and What's Actually at Stake

This is where the Dolphins' season gets interesting in a way the record doesn't show. Miami sits at 1-7, and this isn't an accident — the front office gutted the roster this offseason, and the team has <cite index="47-13">spent a record $188.8 million in dead money this season to clear the way for this new group.</cite> The losing has a side effect that matters: <cite index="50-6,50-7">the Dolphins currently hold the No. 3 position with their 1-7 record, standing behind only the winless Los Angeles Chargers and the Tennessee Titans</cite> in the race for the top of next spring's draft.

Into that picture walks McCord, who Miami <cite index="24-8">traded for in August</cite> after the Eagles drafted him in the sixth round in 2025 and the Packers briefly held his rights. His college résumé is legitimate — <cite index="2-2">McCord went 21-4 as a starter at Ohio State and Syracuse with 61 total touchdown passes</cite> — but none of that answers what happens when he faces NFL pressure for the first time. General manager Jon-Eric Sullivan made the trade with exactly this scenario in mind, saying afterward he felt confident in McCord's ability to keep the offense moving if needed.

Hafley has already shut down any suggestion of a quarterback competition once Willis returns, making clear the job is McCord's to lose for now. For a team that was never going to be judged on wins this year, that's the actual story in Indianapolis on Sunday — not whether Miami climbs out of the bottom of the standings, but whether the quarterback it's turning to by necessity turns into the answer nobody expected to need this soon.
