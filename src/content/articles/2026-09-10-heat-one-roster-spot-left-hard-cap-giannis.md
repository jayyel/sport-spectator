The Miami Heat have 14 players under contract, one open roster spot, and almost no financial room to fill it. That's the state of the team three weeks before training camp opens — a direct consequence of the trade that reshaped the franchise this summer.

## What Giannis actually cost

<cite index="91-2">The Heat acquired superstar forward Giannis Antetokounmpo in a trade with the Milwaukee Bucks on July 6, and it will be his first season with Miami.</cite> The Heat also landed <cite index="91-3">Bobby Portis in the same trade, followed by the signing of veteran guard Klay Thompson in August.</cite> But the return package going the other way was steep. <cite index="81-9,81-10,81-11">Miami sent Tyler Herro, Kel'el Ware, Jaime Jaquez Jr., rookie guard Kasparas Jakucionis, three first-round picks, one pick swap and a second-round pick to Milwaukee, including the No. 13 pick in the 2026 NBA Draft, unprotected first-round picks in 2031 and 2033, a 2030 pick swap and a 2033 second-round selection.</cite>

To make the math work, Miami had to use an unusual trade mechanism. <cite index="79-1">By using the 125 percent expanded trade exception to acquire Antetokounmpo, the Heat are hard-capped below the $209 million first apron, and they will not be able to exceed that number under any circumstance this season.</cite> That single sentence explains almost everything about how quiet Miami's roster has gone since.

The subtractions didn't stop with the trade. <cite index="84-3">The team lost Norman Powell to the Chicago Bulls in free agency</cite>, even as it tried to build shooting and depth back around its two new core pieces. The fix was Klay Thompson, but even that move came with financial gymnastics. <cite index="89-2,89-3">The Dallas Mavericks reached a buyout agreement with Thompson, and he was expected to sign with the Heat on a two-year, $13 million deal with a player option in the second year once he cleared waivers.</cite> Getting there required a personal sacrifice — <cite index="82-3">Thompson gave up about $9.8 million in his Dallas buyout to become a free agent and sign with Miami, according to Michael Scotto of HoopsHype.</cite>

## One spot, three names, no urgency

Around the edges, Miami restocked with modest, cap-friendly additions. <cite index="84-4,84-5">In the last week of free agency the Heat signed Klay Thompson and Nick Richards while also bringing in Tim Hardaway Jr. on a one-year deal earlier in the offseason, and Simone Fontecchio returned to the rotation on a one-year deal.</cite> The draft brought two low-cost additions rather than star power: <cite index="26-4">first-round pick Nate Ament out of Tennessee and second-round pick Otega Oweh out of Kentucky.</cite>

That leaves the roster where it sits now. <cite index="77-1">With 14 of the maximum 15 roster spots filled, the Heat currently have the wherewithal to fill that 15th spot below the hard cap created by the trade for Antetokounmpo.</cite> Filling it isn't required. As one roster breakdown put it, <cite index="80-5,80-6">the Heat don't have to fill their 15th roster spot, though the team still needs help in the backcourt.</cite> Names in circulation include a familiar one: <cite index="80-3,80-4">former All-Star guard Victor Oladipo has received the most consideration among fans, and a Sports Illustrated writer has speculated Oladipo could ultimately land with the Heat.</cite> Other reports have floated <cite index="84-19">37-year-old wing Nicolas Batum, who shot 40.4 percent from three-point range in 74 appearances with the Clippers last season.</cite>

There's a reason nobody in the organization is rushing this. Holding the spot open preserves flexibility for a trade or a buyout-market addition later in the season, and the Heat's hard cap means every dollar committed now is a dollar they can never get back if a better fit surfaces in January.

## The skepticism is real

Not everyone is convinced the pieces fit. <cite index="92-3,92-5,92-6">One NBA analyst argued on The Ringer's NBA show that Miami gave up real depth pieces that let last season's fast-paced offense function, questioning the team's three-point shooting, spacing and offensive creativity going forward.</cite> That's the tension defining Miami's summer: a two-time MVP walking into a building that hasn't had a true franchise centerpiece since LeBron James left, paired with a supporting cast built on minimum contracts and buyout scraps because the front office spent everything else to get him.

Training camp doesn't open until Sept. 29, and the <cite index="49-2">regular season doesn't tip off until Oct. 21</cite>, so there's time for Miami to make a final move. But for now, the story of the Heat's offseason isn't the trade itself — it's the empty locker stall next to it, and how carefully Pat Riley's front office is guarding the last bit of room they have to fill it.
