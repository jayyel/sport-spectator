Bob Heinz, a defensive tackle on the only undefeated team in NFL history, died Saturday of natural causes in Fresno, California. He was 79.

His son John confirmed the death this week. <cite index="19-4">Heinz, who was diagnosed with heart failure four years ago, was 79.</cite> The Dolphins released a statement calling him a key piece of the franchise's run in the early 1970s.

## A Starter on the Only Perfect Team

Heinz was a second-round pick out of the University of the Pacific in the 1969 draft, and he spent nine of his eight NFL seasons in Miami before closing his career with one year in Washington. <cite index="19-5">Heinz, who was listed at 6-6 and 265 pounds during his career, played eight seasons in Miami, appearing in 109 games and starting 55.</cite> He started all 17 games in 1972, the season the Dolphins went unbeaten and won Super Bowl VII, and he was part of the title defense the following year as well. <cite index="19-6">Heinz won two Super Bowls in Miami, in the 1972 and 1973 seasons.</cite>

He wasn't one of the marquee names from that defense — the Dolphins' "No-Name Defense" was built on that kind of anonymity by design — but he anchored the interior line alongside players who became Hall of Famers and franchise legends. After football, he moved into advertising in California and largely stayed out of the spotlight, though he resurfaced briefly in 2015 when he sold one of his championship rings; <cite index="21-7">he made headlines in 2015 when he sold his Super Bowl VIII championship ring to an investor, who later sold it for $68,040.</cite>

## The Roster Keeps Shrinking

Heinz's death matters beyond one obituary because of what it represents for a team that keeps losing the people who made it. Earlier this year, the passing of defensive tackle Manny Fernandez marked a grim threshold: <cite index="38-2">Manny Fernandez dying this week meant that for the first time the Miami Dolphins' 1972 Perfect Season team has fewer living former players than deceased.</cite> At that point, the count stood at two dozen players and coaches who had died against 23 still living. Heinz's death pushes that gap wider.

The living group still includes some of the biggest names in franchise history. Larry Csonka, Bob Griese, Larry Little and Paul Warfield — all Pro Football Hall of Famers from that roster — remain alive, along with safety Dick Anderson, who still lives in South Florida. But the group is aging fast, and the annual ritual of toasting the Perfect Season every time another unbeaten NFL team loses its shot is now a smaller and smaller gathering.

Heinz's family asked that donations in his memory go to the Miami Dolphins Foundation rather than flowers, and a private service is planned. He is survived by his wife, Carol, his three children and two grandchildren.

## A Rough Week to Be Reminded of 1972

The timing lands awkwardly for the current Dolphins, who are 1-6 and sit at the bottom of the AFC East after a Week 8 collapse against New England. The contrast is not lost on anyone who follows the franchise: the only team to ever finish a season without a loss is being remembered in real time while the current roster is trying to avoid a historically bad one of its own.

Miami hosts Detroit on Sunday at Hard Rock Stadium, the same building where the 1972 team was honored on its 50th anniversary four years ago. There's no indication of a formal tribute planned for Heinz before kickoff, but the Dolphins' statement this week suggests the organization is aware of how often it's now making these announcements. The 1972 team remains the standard the franchise is measured against — and every year, there are fewer people left who can say they were actually on the field for it.
