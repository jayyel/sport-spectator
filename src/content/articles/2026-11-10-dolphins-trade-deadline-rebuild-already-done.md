The NFL trade deadline hits at 4 p.m. Eastern today, and the Miami Dolphins are sitting at 1-7 with almost nothing left to trade. That's not an accident. It's the result of a teardown the front office already finished eight months ago.

By the time training camp opened, general manager Jon-Eric Sullivan and the new staff had already moved the two biggest trade chips a selling team could offer. <cite index="40-6">Miami traded veteran safety Minkah Fitzpatrick to the New York Jets in a rare intra-divisional trade at the start of free agency.</cite> Then in March, <cite index="43-6">the trade sent Jaylen Waddle and a 2026 fourth-round pick to Denver</cite> in exchange for a package built around Denver's first-round selection. Add in the earlier split from Mike McDaniel and Tua Tagovailoa, and there wasn't much air left in the roster by the time the 2026 season actually kicked off.

## The Teardown Already Happened

That's the context that makes today feel so anticlimactic. <cite index="91-8">The Dolphins tore their roster down to the studs this past offseason, taking on a record $188.8 million in dead money to set themselves up for future success.</cite> A team that spends like that in March doesn't usually have a second wave of veterans worth moving in November. Miami doesn't.

<cite index="104-11">Even if the Dolphins trade multiple veterans on deadline day, it'll only be a little bit intriguing, since most of Miami's rebuild work was already done months ago.</cite> The names that kept surfacing all week — defensive lineman Zach Sieler, center Aaron Brewer, linebacker Jordyn Brooks — aren't really available. <cite index="105-6">The Dolphins told teams they weren't interested in moving Sieler or center Aaron Brewer, according to one national writer.</cite> Head coach Jeff Hafley backed that up last week, saying <cite index="110-6">he hadn't had any discussions with players about trades at this point</cite>, which is about as clear a signal as a coach gives that the inner circle of the roster isn't moving.

## What's Actually On the Block

The one name that kept recurring, and the one that makes some sense, is right tackle Austin Jackson. <cite index="109-6,109-9">Jackson, the No. 18 overall pick by Miami in the 2020 NFL Draft, has started 67 games at four positions along the Dolphins' offensive line</cite>, and he's in the final year of his contract with uneven play behind him this season. Jackson has handled the speculation with a shrug. <cite index="98-11,98-14">"Same thing I always do. That's not in my control," he said, before adding "Nah, I only heard that from you guys all week"</cite> when asked if last Sunday's loss to Detroit might have been his last game in a Dolphins uniform. Asked more broadly about his future, he put it simply: <cite index="79-9,79-10,79-11">"I want to go wherever wants me," Jackson said. "I love the Dolphins. I've loved my time here."</cite>

Even that market has been shrinking. Sieler's path to a new team took a hit earlier this week — <cite index="104-1">the Jaguars made two different deals for defensive tackles, neither of whom was Sieler, which has pushed the market elsewhere</cite> — and with Sieler and Brewer effectively off the board, Jackson and depth pieces like cornerback JuJu Brents represent most of what Miami has to offer.

## Why the Quiet Is the Point

None of this should read as a front office that's asleep. It reads like one that already made its real decisions back in March, when it had more leverage and a full offseason to work the phones rather than a six-day trade window competing against 31 other teams' deadline business. Waddle at 27 years old with a reasonable contract was worth more in March than any 1-7 team's remaining veteran is worth in November, and Miami cashed that in when the return was biggest.

The quieter version of today's deadline also tells you something about where Sullivan thinks this team actually is. A roster this young, carrying this much dead money, doesn't need to strip down further — it needs reps. <cite index="107-3">The Dolphins are going to need good veteran players beyond 2026, not a team filled entirely with second-, third- or fourth-year players.</cite> Holding onto Sieler, Brewer and Brooks isn't indecision. It's the rebuild entering its next phase, where the point isn't collecting more draft picks — it's figuring out which of the players already here are good enough to keep around when the picks turn into players.

Expect Jackson to be the only name that moves, if anyone moves at all. The real trade deadline for this roster came and went in March.
