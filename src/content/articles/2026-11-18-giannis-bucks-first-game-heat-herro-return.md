Giannis Antetokounmpo has played 13 seasons in the NBA. Every one of them came against the Miami Heat at some point. None of them came against the Milwaukee Bucks — until Wednesday night at Kaseya Center.

The trade that sent Antetokounmpo to Miami became official on July 6. Six and a half months later, the two sides of that deal are standing on the same court. The Bucks arrive at 1-13, losers of 10 straight. The Heat are 8-6, winners of five in a row and 8-2 in their last 10.

## The Trade That Flipped Two Rosters

The framework of the deal never changed much from when rumors started. <cite index="40-1,40-3">The Milwaukee Bucks traded franchise icon Giannis Antetokounmpo and Bobby Portis to the Heat for Tyler Herro, Kel'el Ware, Jaime Jaquez Jr., Kasparas Jakucionis and draft compensation.</cite> Antetokounmpo had made it known Miami was a preferred destination, and the two front offices worked the framework for months before completing it.

Six and a half months later, the results could not look more different. <cite index="59-7,59-8">The Bucks are 1-13, having lost 10 straight, while the Heat are 8-6, going 8-2 in their last 10.</cite> Milwaukee fired its coaching staff and brought in Taylor Jenkins, and the team is off to its worst start in years with the four incoming players from Miami still finding their footing. Meanwhile Miami's gamble has paid off early — the farmhand package Pat Riley assembled hasn't been tested the way a true contender would test it, but the Heat haven't needed to replace what they gave up because what they got back has been otherworldly.

## Giannis Hasn't Needed a Reunion to Look Like Himself

Through his first 14 games in Miami, <cite index="60-5,60-6">Antetokounmpo is averaging 28.3 points, 12.5 rebounds and 5.2 assists on 60.1% shooting, with 11 double-doubles already banked, leading the Heat in scoring, rebounding and blocks.</cite> He strung together a stretch of 30-plus point double-doubles in mid-November, including 37 points against Cleveland and 34 against Detroit, and he followed that with 20 points, 13 rebounds and six assists in Monday's win over Houston.

Antetokounmpo has downplayed the emotional weight of facing the organization that drafted him, signed him to every contract of his career, and won a championship with him in 2021. Asked about it this week, he said bluntly: <cite index="58-1,58-2,58-3,58-4">"Just another game. I'm gonna do the same thing, eat the same meal. I'm going to kiss my kids. It's the same thing."</cite> Those who have actually walked that path before tend to describe it differently once the ball goes up.

## The Four Who Went the Other Way

The return trip matters just as much. <cite index="58-8,58-9">The four former Heat players traded for Antetokounmpo — Tyler Herro, Jaime Jaquez Jr., Kel'el Ware and Kasparas Jakucionis — are back in Miami with a team that has lost 10 games in a row, and 13 of 14 overall.</cite> None of them have had especially smooth going in Milwaukee. <cite index="58-10,58-11">Herro was benched for the fourth quarter in the Bucks' last outing, Jaquez hasn't started the past three games, Ware's minutes are below where they were last season, and only Jakucionis — the player Milwaukee specifically insisted be included in the deal — has gotten more time of late.</cite>

Miami is planning to honor Herro specifically. <cite index="61-6">The team plans to show a video tribute for its former players returning with Milwaukee, centered on Tyler Herro,</cite> who spent seven seasons as a homegrown Heat fixture before the trade ended his run in South Florida in a matter of minutes last July. Herro has been careful in public about how he frames the breakup, saying of his old running mate early in the season: <cite index="42-6,42-7,42-8">"I don't think we're replacing him. Obviously he's the greatest Buck that's played for the organization. But we're not replacing him."</cite>

The game itself doubles as a marketing spectacle — Miami has turned the night into a "Vice City" promotion tied to the release of a video game set in a fictionalized version of South Florida, complete with a redesigned court and a limited-edition jersey drop. That's the kind of thing that gets attention on a Wednesday in November. The basketball underneath it is the real story: a two-time MVP who no longer wears the uniform that made him famous, a player development staff in Milwaukee trying to justify giving him up, and four young players walking into the building where they used to be the plan, not the return on one.

For Miami, the stakes of the night are modest — one more game in a winning streak against a team near the bottom of the conference. For Milwaukee, it's a measuring stick for whether the return package can become anything more than a very expensive rebuild. The scoreboard from the regular-season standings already suggests which side got the better end of July's trade. Wednesday just puts it on the same court.
