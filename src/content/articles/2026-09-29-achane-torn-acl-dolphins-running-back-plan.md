De'Von Achane's season ended on his third carry of the day. He picked up 11 yards on Miami's opening drive against Kansas City, got up, and stood still while teammates gathered around him. Two days and a battery of tests later, the Dolphins confirmed what everyone in Miami Gardens feared watching him walk gingerly to the medical tent: a torn ACL, an injured reserve stint, and a season now missing its best offensive player before October even starts.

This is the same running back the franchise handed a four-year, $64 million extension to over the summer. The same one head coach Jeff Hafley half-joked in the preseason he wanted touching the ball 40 to 45 times a game, telling Achane to get in shape because there might be nights the coaching staff had to carry him to the locker room. Instead, Achane finishes 2026 with 34 carries for 127 yards, seven catches for 49 yards, and zero touchdowns. Hafley called it Monday, saying <cite index="66-3">"My heart breaks for him but at the same time, I know that he'll be back and he'll be back strong."</cite>

## A Rebuild That Just Lost Its Floor

The injury lands on a team that has nowhere to absorb it. Through three weeks, Miami ranks 31st in the league in scoring at 12 points a game and 26th in total yardage, and that was with Achane on the field for most of it. He wasn't just Miami's best skill player — he was the only proven, established offensive weapon left after an offseason that saw Tyreek Hill hit free agency and Jaylen Waddle traded to Denver. Malik Willis is starting at quarterback. The receiving room is built on rookies and depth pieces. Achane was the one component of the offense defenses had to genuinely fear, and now he's gone for good.

Hafley insists the plan doesn't change. <cite index="66-7,66-8,66-9">"That philosophy remains -- I want to run the football. That's important and we've got the offensive line to do it. I have a lot of confidence in these backs, so this isn't like we can't run the ball anymore,"</cite> he said Monday. That's the company line a first-year coach has to give reporters two days after his best player tore his ACL. It's also not really true. Nobody drafts a scheme around Ollie Gordon II and Jaylen Wright the way they draft one around a Pro Bowl back who averages six yards a touch.

## The Committee Nobody Wanted

What Miami actually has now is a backfield built on stopgaps and injury questions of its own. Wright, the presumptive No. 2 back, was inactive Sunday with a stinger and a foot injury and remains day-to-day. Gordon, a second-year pro who'd totaled three carries for seven yards over the first two games, stepped in when Achane went down and ran 17 times for 41 yards — 2.4 yards a carry — before cramping late in the third quarter. He scored a touchdown and caught three passes, which is the kind of stat line that looks fine in a box score and unconvincing on tape.

Hafley's answer, when asked whether the Dolphins would move to a committee approach, was blunt in its vagueness: <cite index="65-1">"They're all going to have their roles."</cite> That's not a depth chart, it's a hope. The team's actual depth chart this week lists Wright and Gordon at RB with nothing behind Achane's old spot, and receiver Malik Washington listed as the emergency third option — a role Hafley says was intentional given his confidence in Washington's ability to fill in if truly needed. General manager Jon-Eric Sullivan is reportedly weighing whether to add another back from outside the building before Sunday.

None of the options fix the real issue. Wright has averaged 5.5 carries a game across two NFL seasons and has never been trusted with a full workload. Gordon looked overwhelmed in his first real test. The Dolphins traded receiver Tutu Atwell to the Rams over the summer partly to acquire running back Jarquez Hunter, who has yet to record a single offensive touch in his career. That's the cavalry.

## Why This Is the Story, Not the Loss to Kansas City

Miami dropping to 0-3 against a Chiefs team that controlled most of Sunday's game was not surprising. Losing the one player capable of making this offense functional in year one of a rebuild is a different kind of setback — it's the kind that turns a difficult season into a lost one before the calendar hits October. Hafley can talk about total confidence in his backs all he wants. The tape from Sunday, and the collective résumé of Wright, Gordon and Hunter, say the Dolphins are now auditioning replacement parts for a player who wasn't supposed to need replacing until 2030.

The bigger question for Miami isn't who leads the backfield against Cincinnati this week. It's whether an 0-3 team with a rookie-heavy receiver room and a downgraded backfield has any real path to competitive relevance this season, or whether Sunday's injury just accelerated the only thing that was ever actually on the agenda: evaluating Willis, developing the offensive line, and waiting for 2027.
